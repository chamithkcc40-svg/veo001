## Draft script

# Cold open
Listen. A siren races toward you. And the moment it passes, its note drops. That little drop is how we found out the whole universe is growing.

# Chapter 1 - The Note That Bends
Picture the ambulance driver. To her, the siren plays one steady note, the whole time. But you, on the sidewalk, hear it high as it comes, then low as it goes. Same siren. Two different sounds. And you're both right. So what is actually changing? Not the siren. Something in the air between you.

# Chapter 2 - Ripples
Sound travels as waves, like ripples on a pond. Now picture a bug sitting still on that pond, tapping the water. Perfect rings spread out evenly in every direction. But let the bug swim forward while it taps. The rings in front get squeezed together. The rings behind it get stretched apart. Sound does exactly this. Squeezed waves hit your ear more often, so the pitch sounds higher. Stretched waves arrive less often, so it sounds lower. The waves never speed up. Only their spacing changes. A teacher in Prague named Christian Doppler worked this out in eighteen forty-two. He was frail, overworked, and had nearly given up and moved to America. Three years later, a Dutch scientist tested his idea with horn players on an open train car. People beside the track heard the notes bend. But here's the strange part. Doppler's paper wasn't really about sound. It was about the stars.

# Chapter 3 - Light Has a Siren Too
Light is a wave too. And for light, the spacing of the waves is its color. Squeeze light waves and they shift toward blue. Stretch them and they shift toward red. That's a redshift. We never notice it in daily life, because light is about a million times faster than sound. But across space, it shows. Starlight carries a barcode of dark lines, set by the atoms that made it. If the barcode slides toward red, that object is moving away. In the nineteen tens, an astronomer at Lowell Observatory named Vesto Slipher read those barcodes in fuzzy spirals in the sky. Andromeda was coming toward us, at about three hundred kilometers every second. But nearly all the others were running away. Why would everything be running from us?

# Chapter 4 - The Running Universe
Enter Edwin Hubble. In nineteen twenty-three, with the biggest telescope of his time, he proved those spirals were whole galaxies, far beyond our own. Then he measured how far away they were. In nineteen twenty-nine, he set his distances beside Slipher's speeds. And a pattern jumped out. The farther a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away. Every galaxy, running from every other. The universe is expanding. A Belgian priest, Georges Lemaitre, had predicted this two years earlier. But Hubble's chart is what convinced the world. And there's one more twist. The galaxies aren't really racing through space. Space itself is stretching, like rising bread pulling raisins apart. The light stretches with it, all the way to red.

# Close
So the next time a siren drops its note as it passes you, listen closely. You're hearing the same trick that told us the universe is still growing.
