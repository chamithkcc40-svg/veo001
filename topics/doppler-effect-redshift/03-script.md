# Cold open
Listen. A siren races toward you. The moment it passes, its note drops. That tiny drop holds the clue that told us the whole universe is growing.

# Chapter 1 - One Siren, Two Sounds
Picture the ambulance driver. To her, the siren plays one steady note. But you, on the sidewalk, hear it high as it comes, and low as it goes. Same siren. Two sounds. And you're both right. So what changed? Not the siren. Something in the air between you.

# Chapter 2 - Ripples
Sound travels in waves, like ripples on a pond. Picture a bug sitting still, tapping the water. Perfect rings spread out evenly. Now the bug swims forward while it taps. The rings in front get squeezed together. The rings behind get stretched apart. Sound does the same. Squeezed waves reach your ear more often, so the pitch sounds higher. Stretched waves arrive less often, so it sounds lower. The waves never speed up. Only their spacing changes. In eighteen forty-two, a frail math professor in Prague worked this out. His name was Christian Doppler, and he had once nearly quit and moved to America. Three years later, a Dutch scientist put horn players on an open train car. People beside the track heard the notes bend. But here's the twist. Doppler didn't name his paper after sound. He named it after the colored light of stars.

# Chapter 3 - Light Has a Siren Too
Light is a wave too, and for light, spacing means color. Squeeze the waves, and light shifts toward blue. Stretch them, and it shifts toward red. That's a redshift. Doppler guessed wrong about star colors. The shift was far too small to notice. But he predicted astronomers would one day use it to measure how stars move. They did. Starlight carries a barcode of dark lines, set by its atoms. If the barcode slides toward red, the source is moving away. In the nineteen tens, Vesto Slipher read those barcodes in faint spirals in the sky. Andromeda was coming toward us, about three hundred kilometers every second. But nearly all the others were rushing away. Why would almost everything flee from us?

# Chapter 4 - The Running Universe
Enter Edwin Hubble. In nineteen twenty-three, with the most powerful telescope of its day, he proved Andromeda was a whole galaxy, far beyond our own. Next, he measured how far away other galaxies were. In nineteen twenty-nine, he set his distances beside their speeds, most of them Slipher's. A pattern jumped out. The farther a galaxy is, the faster it moves away. Not just from us. From each other. The universe is expanding. A Belgian priest, Georges Lemaître, had proposed this two years earlier. But Hubble's chart is what convinced astronomers. And one last twist. Far galaxies aren't really speeding through space like sirens down a street. Space itself is stretching, like rising dough pulling raisins apart. Their light stretches with it, toward red.

# Close
So the next time a siren drops its note as it passes, listen. You're hearing the same clue that told us the universe is growing.
